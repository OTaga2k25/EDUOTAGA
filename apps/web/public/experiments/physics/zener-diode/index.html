Zener diode: I–V characteristic and reverse breakdown voltage

A Zener diode is made to work in reverse bias. Below its breakdown voltage almost no current flows. At the Zener voltage VZ the current rises sharply while the voltage across the diode stays nearly constant. Find VZ and see how a Zener regulates voltage.

Forward bias: the p side of the Zener diode is connected to the positive terminal. The series resistor limits the current.

The idea

A Zener diode is a heavily doped p-n junction with a thin depletion layer. In forward bias it behaves like an ordinary silicon diode, conducting after about 0.7 V. In reverse bias only a tiny current flows until the reverse voltage reaches the Zener (breakdown) voltage VZ. Then the strong electric field across the thin junction pulls electrons out of their bonds (Zener breakdown), and the reverse current rises sharply.

After breakdown the voltage across the diode stays almost constant at VZ for a wide range of current. Any extra supply voltage drops across the series resistor. This is why a Zener diode is used as a voltage regulator.

  • Zener voltage: extend the steep breakdown part of the reverse curve to meet the V axis
  • Series resistor: Vsupply = VZ + I R, so it limits the current after breakdown
  • Dynamic (Zener) resistance: rZ = ΔV / ΔI after breakdown; small, a few ohms

    Precautions

    • Always connect a series resistor, so that the current after breakdown stays within the safe rating of the Zener diode.
    • In reverse bias the n side (the end with the band) goes to the positive terminal.
    • Increase the voltage slowly near breakdown, where the current rises sharply, and take more readings there.
    • Do not let the current exceed the maximum rated value, or the diode will overheat.
    • Use a voltmeter of suitable range across the diode only, not across the resistor.
