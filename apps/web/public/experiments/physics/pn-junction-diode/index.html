p-n junction diode: forward and reverse bias characteristics

Increase the voltage across a diode and read the current. In forward bias the current stays tiny until the knee voltage, then rises steeply. In reverse bias only a few microamperes flow. Find the knee voltage, the forward and reverse resistances, and tell whether the diode is silicon or germanium.

Forward bias: the p side of the diode is connected to the positive terminal. The protective resistor limits the current.

The idea

A p-n junction has a depletion layer with a small potential barrier. In forward bias (p side to +) the applied voltage lowers the barrier. Very little current flows until the voltage reaches the barrier potential, the knee (cut-in) voltage: about 0.7 V for silicon and 0.3 V for germanium. Beyond it the current rises steeply. In reverse bias the barrier grows, and only a tiny reverse saturation current of minority carriers flows (nanoamperes for silicon, microamperes for germanium).

  • Knee voltage: extend the straight, steep part of the forward curve to meet the V axis
  • Forward (dynamic) resistance: rf = ΔV / ΔI on the steep part (a few ohms)
  • Reverse resistance: rr = ΔV / ΔI on the reverse curve (megaohms)

Precautions

  • Always use a protective resistor in forward bias, or the current can rise enough to damage the diode.
  • Use a milliammeter in forward bias and a microammeter in reverse bias. Check the polarity of the meters.
  • Near the knee the current changes quickly, so increase the voltage in small steps there.
  • Do not exceed the safe current in forward bias, and keep the reverse voltage well below the breakdown voltage.
  • Take readings with the key closed only for a short time, so that the diode does not heat up.
